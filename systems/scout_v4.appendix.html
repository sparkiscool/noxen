The Scout looks into the ceiling on a tape‑measure mast

The drone flies up to 12 cm under the ceiling grid. Two steel tape blades, curves facing so they lock into a stiff tube, roll 30 cm up out of the roof and lift a 5 MP autofocus camera through an open tile into the ceiling void. The drone yaws to pan, a servo tilts, and every pipe, conduit, fitting and cable run is photographed from close range. Parked, the same head is the Scout's only camera, filming 1080p at 30 fps over the nose. The probe weighs about 55 g and its parts cost about $93.

How ceiling voids are inspected today

Nobody flies a drone into a drop-ceiling void as a routine product. Inspectors push a camera up from below, crawl a robot across the tiles, or fly a large caged drone in open industrial ceilings. Each one teaches something for the Scout.

What the Scout's ceiling demands

The void

Suspended ceilings hang on a grid of 600 mm (2 ft) tiles. The void above is typically 150–600 mm deep, and 600–900 mm in ducted offices (clearance guide). It holds sprinkler mains and branches, ducts, conduit with couplings and junction boxes, cable trays, and the hanger wires that hold the grid. It is dark and dusty.

The ceiling effect

A rotor close under a surface gains thrust. Research finds the effect smaller and steadier than ground effect (indoor airflow study), but a flight controller that holds altitude keeps adding thrust when the drone stops climbing, and pilots report quads being sucked onto the ceiling (forum report). Prop wash also lifts loose tiles. Spark's call is that the Scout can fly close, so it holds its roof 120 mm below the grid. Its props are then 137 mm below, about one prop diameter, where the effect is noticeable but modest, and the mast does the rest of the reaching.

Where a mast can stand

Every part of the Scout keeps 12 mm from the swept 5.1 in prop discs. A vertical mast clears all four only within about 24 mm of the drone's centre on the diagonals, and 43 mm on the axes. The centre is also where yawing spins the head in place. So the probe lives in the flat roof's middle node, with its spool hanging under it above the FC. At its corners the spool is 15 mm from the discs.

Weight and money

The Scout is already about 735 g (E) on 5.1 in props. Spark's limit for this probe is cheap and light. The design keeps to parts under $40 each and adds no second pan axis, because the drone's own yaw is a free pan axis.

What the camera records

The camera is the Arducam UB0238, a 5 MP autofocus board with the OmniVision OV5648 sensor, on the Radxa over USB 2.0. Its sister board, the B0441 first proposed, records 1080p at only 5 fps, so it was swapped out.

The mast, part by part

The turret sits under the roof's middle node, where the flight controller used to be. The controller moves to a bay under the nose. Everything that has to stand in the props' height band is within 24 mm of the centre, the only place a part stays 12 mm from all four props. The gear motor lies in a pocket in the deck, below that band.

  1. Two blades. Two 10 mm steel tape blades, 0.4 m each with 0.3 m used, with their curves facing. Out of the throat they spring into a lens-shaped tube, and each braces the direction the other folds easily. This is the bi-STEM idea from spacecraft booms. The flat cable runs inside the lens, protected.
  2. One spool. Both blades and the cable wind flat together on one printed spool: hub Ø30, about Ø33 wound, Ø34 flanges, axis across the drone at 43 mm above the deck's datum. About 4 turns pay out 0.4 m.
  3. Drive. An N20 300 rpm gear motor with encoder lies in a pocket in the deck and turns the spool through a printed 15 / 40 tooth pair (module 0.8, centres 22 mm apart). 0.3 m takes about 1.6 s. The encoder gives the length in millimetres.
  4. Clock spring. The spool turns, so the cable can't leave from its axle. Beside the spool, a 300 mm flat-cable spiral in a drum tightens and loosens like a watch spring. It has room for about 6 turns and the mast uses about 4.
  5. Throat. A 30 mm printed guide beside the roof's middle node. It takes the blades off the spool and curls them into the lens, and it grips the mast's base, where a knock bends it hardest. The middle node becomes a ring around it; the four roof rods end in the ring's sides.
  6. Head. A clip on the blades' tip, an SG90 servo on its side to tilt from 20° down to straight up, a cradle with the camera board, 2–4 white LEDs and a VL53L0X distance sensor beside the lens, and a thin TPU bumper. Parked, it rests on the throat's top with its lens 90 mm above the deck's datum, looking forward 20° up.

If something hits the mast

  • The two-blade tube is far harder to fold than one blade, and the 30 mm throat holds its base.
  • The bumper makes a knock glance off and flexes before the blades do.
  • The distance sensor stops the yaw before the head is within 60 mm of anything.
  • If the view suddenly jumps, the software rolls the mast in, and the throat straightens the blades on the way down.
  • The blades are turned so that a fold falls along the roof's centre rods, not into an open roof panel over a prop.

A scan at one opening

  1. Fly under the open tile, centred by the flow sensor; the probe's distance sensor sees the opening as far and the tiles as near.
  2. Hold the roof 120 mm below the grid.
  3. Roll the tape out 200 mm, until the head is 90 mm above the grid, LED on.
  4. At 20° down, 15° up, 55° up and straight up, yaw a full turn in 30° steps and take a still at each: 37 stills.
  5. Roll out the full 300 mm, to 190 mm above the grid, and repeat: 74 stills in about 2 minutes of hover at 1.5 s per still. In a deep void the 55° and 90° tilts reach the slab and the pipes on it.
  6. Roll the mast back until the head rests on the throat, and fly to the next opening.

From stills to a map

Each still is saved with its yaw, tilt, tape length and 8 × 8 distances. Free photogrammetry software such as COLMAP or Meshroom turns a set into a 3D model of the void, with the distances setting its scale. The stills are still worth having without the model: every fitting is in at least two of them.

Wiring and control

The probe camera goes on the Radxa Zero 3W's second USB-C port in host mode, since the Radxa is powered from its pads. The distance sensor, servo and motor driver run from the Radxa's I²C and PWM pins. The scan runs on the Radxa and asks PX4 for each yaw step over MAVLink. Power comes from the UBEC's 5 V, at about 0.5 A peak with the motor and servo running.

One camera for the whole Scout

Parked, the head rests on the throat with its lens at (62, 0, 90.5) mm in the drone's frame, about 30 mm above the roof rods. Tilted 20° up, its 65 × 51° frame spans 5.5° below the horizon to 45.5° above it, and no rod or prop is in the picture. The servo tilts it further up for ceilings. The old DECXIN camera and its posts are gone.

  • Gained: autofocus, 5 MP stills, 1080p at 30 fps, one USB camera on the Radxa.
  • Given up: a 65° view instead of about 89°, a rolling shutter that smears fast video, and a second camera if the head is damaged.

How we build it

Each step ends with a test that has to pass before the next step. The mast and the cable are the two unknowns, so they are proven on the bench before anything flies.

  1. Order the parts in the cheapest-parts table, about $93, plus a spare tape and a spare cable set.
  2. Draw the mechanism parts in the repo. The spool with its gear, the pinion, the drum, the housing and throat, the cradle and the clip are fits and slots, not loaded structure, so each is a drawn part with its own make.py, like the Overseer's TPU foot. Print them in PCTG, and the lip and bumper in TPU 95A.
  3. Build the mast on the bench. Cut 0.4 m from each tape, drill a 2 mm hole at each end, clamp both blades to the spool hub curves facing, and fit the gears and the N20. Pass: 500 roll-outs with no jam, the lens fully formed above the throat, and a 20 g dummy head at 0.3 m settling within a second after a tap. Then push the head sideways with a spring scale until it folds, and do the same with one blade, so the gain is a measured number.
  4. Prove the cable. Lay the flat cable between the blades and fold the drum's spiral. Plug the camera into the Radxa through it and stream 1080p for 10 minutes while the mast cycles 100 times. Pass: no USB drop-out. If it fails, switch to a thin coiled USB cable beside the blade.
  5. Assemble the head. Fit the servo, camera board, LEDs and distance sensor on the cradle. Pass: autofocus sharp at 10 cm, 0.5 m and 2 m, and a usable still in a dark box with the LEDs on.
  6. Write the probe service on the Radxa. Home by retracting until the head seats on the lip (the encoder stalls), extend to a length in millimetres, stop the yaw when the distance sensor reads under 60 mm, and save each still with its yaw, tilt, length and 8 × 8 distances. When parked, stream the front view.
  7. Write the scan. The Radxa asks PX4 over MAVLink for each 30° yaw step and waits for it to settle before each still. Run it in PX4's simulator first, then on the bench with the props off.
  8. Fit it to v4. The roof's middle node is designed with the turret's mount when the cage nodes are designed. Bolt the turret on, wire it to the Radxa and the UBEC, weigh the Scout and check the centre of gravity.
  9. Fly under a test ceiling. Hang a 2 × 2 tile grid with one tile open over a frame. Hover 30 cm under it, then 20, then 12, logging thrust and altitude. Pass: no pull toward the grid. Then roll the tape out in the hover, run a full scan, and build a COLMAP model from the stills.
  10. Fly a real ceiling, one opening first.

What to prove first

  • Ceiling effect at 12 cm. The props are about one diameter below the grid. Test the hover there first. If PX4 starts climbing into the ceiling, hold a few centimetres lower and roll the tape out further.
  • Folding. The two-blade tube should take several times the push of one blade. That is a judgement until the bench test measures it.
  • Gear mesh. A printed module 0.8 pair needs its centre distance right to 0.1 mm. Print the housing with the two shaft holes in one piece.
  • USB over flat cable. USB 2.0 over 0.6 m of unshielded flat cable should work, but it is unshielded. Test the camera through the rolled ribbon; the fallback is a thin coiled USB cable beside the tape.
  • Snagging. The head must not catch a pipe or hanger wire while the drone yaws. The distance sensor stops the yaw under 60 mm, and the yaw runs slowly.
  • Hover time. The probe is about 55 g, but dropping the runners and keel saves more, so the Scout is about 697 g all-up (E), with about 2 minutes of hover per opening.
  • Parking. The parked head is the highest point of the Scout. Its TPU bumper takes an upside-down landing first.

The probe touches nothing below the deck, so the funnel seat and the battery swapper do not change.
