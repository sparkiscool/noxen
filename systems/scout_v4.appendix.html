The Scout looks into the ceiling on a tape‑measure mast

The drone flies up to 12 cm under the ceiling grid. Two steel tape blades, curves facing so they lock into a stiff tube, roll 15 cm up out of the roof and lift a colour camera through an open tile into the ceiling void. The drone yaws to pan, a servo tilts, and every pipe, conduit, fitting and cable run is photographed from close range. Parked, the same head is the Scout's only camera, filming 1080p in colour over the nose and taking 12 MP stills in the ceiling. The probe weighs about 61 g, and the parts still to buy cost about $30: the camera is already bought.

How ceiling voids are inspected today

Nobody flies a drone into a drop-ceiling void as a routine product. Inspectors push a camera up from below, crawl a robot across the tiles, or fly a large caged drone in open industrial ceilings. Each one teaches something for the Scout.

What the Scout's ceiling demands

The void

Suspended ceilings hang on a grid of 600 mm (2 ft) tiles. The void above is typically 150–600 mm deep, and 600–900 mm in ducted offices (clearance guide). It holds sprinkler mains and branches, ducts, conduit with couplings and junction boxes, cable trays, and the hanger wires that hold the grid. It is dark and dusty.

The ceiling effect

A rotor close under a surface gains thrust. Research finds the effect smaller and steadier than ground effect (indoor airflow study), but a flight controller that holds altitude keeps adding thrust when the drone stops climbing, and pilots report quads being sucked onto the ceiling (forum report). Prop wash also lifts loose tiles. Spark's call is that the Scout can fly close, so it holds its roof 120 mm below the grid. Its props are then 137 mm below, about one prop diameter, where the effect is noticeable but modest, and the mast does the rest of the reaching.

Where a mast can stand

Every part of the Scout keeps 12 mm from the swept 5.1 in prop discs. A vertical mast clears all four only within about 24 mm of the drone's centre on the diagonals, and 43 mm on the axes. The centre is also where yawing spins the head in place. So the probe lives in the flat roof's middle node, with its spool hanging under it above the FC. At its corners the spool is 15 mm from the discs.

Weight and money

The Scout is already about 735 g (E) on 5.1 in props. Spark's limit for this probe is cheap and light. The design keeps every part but the camera under $16 and adds no second pan axis, because the drone's own yaw is a free pan axis.

What the camera records

The camera is the one Spark bought: the Arducam 12MP autofocus USB camera (B0FLX58WKZ). It is a standard USB video camera, so the Radxa Zero 3W's Linux takes it with no driver. It weighs 45 g in its metal case; on the mast it flies as the bare board, about 12 g (an estimate until it is weighed).

The mast, part by part

The turret sits under the roof's middle node, where the flight controller used to be. The controller moves to a bay under the nose. Everything that has to stand in the props' height band is within 24 mm of the centre, the only place a part stays 12 mm from all four props. The gear motor lies in a pocket in the deck, below that band.

  1. Two blades. Two 19 mm (¾") steel tape blades, cut 0.2 m from each of two ordinary 3 m tape measures, 0.15 m used, with their curves facing. 19 mm rather than 10 (Spark: "as safe as possible"): about 3.5 times the bending moment to fold them, for about 5 g more, so the mast holds the camera even in its case. Out of the throat they spring into a lens-shaped tube, and each braces the direction the other folds easily. This is the bi-STEM idea from spacecraft booms. The flat cable runs inside the lens, protected. The reach is as short as still works (Spark: "as safe as possible"): halving it from 0.3 m makes the tip about 8 times stiffer, raises the load that buckles it about 4 times and halves the bending at the throat, and the lens still ends about 7.5 cm above the grid, roughly 6 cm over the tiles' tops.
  2. One spool. Both blades and the cable wind flat together on one printed spool: hub Ø30 and 21 mm wide, about Ø33 wound, Ø34 flanges, axis across the drone at 43 mm above the deck's datum. About 1.5 turns pay out the 0.15 m.
  3. Drive. An N20 300 rpm gear motor lies in a pocket in the deck and turns the spool through a printed 15 / 40 tooth pair (module 0.8, centres 22 mm apart). The 0.15 m takes about 0.8 s. It needs no encoder: the blades' ends are fixed to the spool, so fully out is a hard stop, and fully in the head seats on the throat. The Radxa runs the motor to the stall at either end and cuts the power.
  4. Cable. The camera's own USB cable does the whole run, with no flat cable or adapter boards. It goes up between the two blades, inside the lens, winds on the spool beside them, and leaves at the axle into a drum where it lies in a loose coil. The coil has room for 2.5 turns and the spool makes about 1.5, so it tightens and loosens like a watch spring. At the fixed end it plugs into the Radxa through the USB-C plug board Spark already has.
  5. Throat. A 30 mm printed guide beside the roof's middle node. It takes the blades off the spool and curls them into the lens, and it grips the mast's base, where a knock bends it hardest. The middle node becomes a ring around it; the four roof rods end in the ring's sides.
  6. Head. A clip with an M2 screw through both blades' tips holds a small base plate. On its +y side a bracket holds an SG90 by its tabs, lying on its side with its shaft across the drone; on its -y side a second bracket holds an M3 screw on the same line as the idler. Between them the tilt cradle hangs on the servo's horn and the screw: two arms, a back plate with four M2 posts for the camera's bare board, and a short printed hood round the lens. The servo nods the whole cradle from 20° down to straight up; it clears the base plate by 2.7 mm and the servo and brackets everywhere in that range (checked in tools/scout_v4_shape.py). Parked, it rests on the throat with its lens about 106 mm above the deck's datum, looking forward 15° up.
  7. Light. Two 32 mm bars cut from a 5 V COB LED strip (CRI 90+, 4000–5000 K), one each side of the lens and 20–27 mm out from its axis, facing the way the camera looks. Two sides fill the shadows behind pipes; keeping the light off the lens's axis keeps lit dust out of the picture; the hood keeps the bars' own light out of the lens; and the strip's continuous line gives soft highlights on ducts and copper instead of glare spots. About 1 W together (about 100 lm, E): roughly 130 lux at 0.5 m, enough for 1/120 s frames. The bars nod with the camera, so the light always points where it looks.

If something hits the mast

  • The two-blade tube is far harder to fold than one blade, the blades are 19 mm wide (about 3.5 times the fold moment of 10 mm ones), and the 30 mm throat holds its base.
  • The TPU guard on the camera board's rim makes a knock glance off before it loads the blades.
  • The yaw runs slowly, and the software stops it when the autofocus says something is within about 10 cm.
  • If the view suddenly jumps, the software rolls the mast in, and the throat straightens the blades on the way down.
  • The blades are turned so that a fold falls along the roof's centre rods, not into an open roof panel over a prop.

A scan at one opening

  1. Fly under the open tile, centred by the flow sensor; the probe's distance sensor sees the opening as far and the tiles as near.
  2. Hold the roof 120 mm below the grid.
  3. Roll the tape out its 150 mm, until the lens is about 75 mm above the grid, LEDs on.
  4. Nod down to 5° and yaw a full turn in 45° steps, holding still about 0.2 s for a 12 MP frame at each; nod up to 45° and turn again; then one frame straight up: 17 frames in about 40 s of hover. The video runs the whole time as well.
  5. Roll the mast back until the head rests on the throat, and fly to the next opening.

From stills to a map

Each still is saved with its yaw, tilt and the autofocus's focus distance. Free photogrammetry software such as COLMAP or Meshroom turns a set into a 3D model of the void, with the focus distances setting its scale. The stills are still worth having without the model: every fitting is in at least two of them.

Wiring and control

The probe camera goes on the Radxa Zero 3W's second USB-C port in host mode, through the USB-C plug board, since the Radxa is powered from its pads. The servo and the motor driver run from the Radxa's PWM and GPIO pins. The scan runs on the Radxa and asks PX4 for each yaw step over MAVLink. Power comes from the UBEC's 5 V, at about 0.5 A peak with the motor and servo running.

One camera for the whole Scout

Parked, the head rests on the throat with its lens at about (34, 0, 106) mm in the drone's frame. Tilted 15° up, its 61 × 50° frame spans 10° below the horizon to 40° above it, and no rod or prop is in the picture. The servo tilts it up for ceilings.

  • Gained: colour, 12 MP stills with very fine detail, and autofocus from 10 cm, so a fitting right in front of the lens is sharp.
  • Given up: a wide view (61° across, so 17 frames a scan instead of 13), a global shutter (the scan holds still for each frame), and the case: the bare board needs the TPU guard on its rim.

How we build it

Each step ends with a test that has to pass before the next step. The mast and the cable are the two unknowns, so they are proven on the bench before anything flies.

  1. Order the parts in the cheapest-parts table, about $30, plus a spare tape.
  2. Draw the mechanism parts in the repo. The spool with its gear, the pinion, the drum, the housing and throat, the cradle and the clip are fits and slots, not loaded structure, so each is a drawn part with its own make.py, like the Overseer's TPU foot. Print them in PCTG, and the lip and bumper in TPU 95A.
  3. Build the mast on the bench. Cut 0.2 m from each tape, drill a 2 mm hole at each end, clamp both blades to the spool hub curves facing, and fit the gears and the N20. Pass: 500 roll-outs with no jam, the lens fully formed above the throat, and a 60 g dummy head (the camera in its case) at 0.15 m settling within a second after a tap. Then push the head sideways with a luggage scale until the mast folds. Pass: at least 0.2 N·m at the throat, about 1.3 N at the head, three times the worst load from the drone's tilting and jerking with the case on.
  4. Prove the cable. Take the camera out of its case, run its own USB cable between the blades, round the spool and into the drum's coil, and plug it into the Radxa through the USB-C plug board. Stream 1080p for 10 minutes while the mast cycles 100 times. Pass: no USB drop-out and no chafe on the cable's jacket.
  5. Assemble the head. Measure the bare board (size, holes, lens height) and fit the cradle's posts to it, then fit the servo, the camera and the LED bars. Pass: autofocus sharp at 0.1, 0.5 and 1 m, and a usable frame in a dark box with the LEDs on.
  6. Write the probe service on the Radxa. Retract until the motor stalls with the head seated, extend until it stalls at the blades' anchor (time-limited, about 1 s each way), stop the yaw when the autofocus reads under 10 cm, and save each still with its yaw, tilt and focus distance. When parked, stream the front view.
  7. Write the scan. The Radxa asks PX4 over MAVLink for each 60° yaw step and waits for it to settle before each still. Run it in PX4's simulator first, then on the bench with the props off.
  8. Fit it to v4. The roof's middle node is designed with the turret's mount when the cage nodes are designed. Bolt the turret on, wire it to the Radxa and the UBEC, weigh the Scout and check the centre of gravity.
  9. Fly under a test ceiling. Hang a 2 × 2 tile grid with one tile open over a frame. Hover 30 cm under it, then 20, then 12, logging thrust and altitude. Pass: no pull toward the grid. Then roll the tape out in the hover, run a full scan, and build a COLMAP model from the stills.
  10. Fly a real ceiling, one opening first.

What to prove first

  • Ceiling effect at 12 cm. The props are about one diameter below the grid. Test the hover there first. If PX4 starts climbing into the ceiling, hold a few centimetres lower and roll the tape out further.
  • Folding. The two-blade tube should take several times the push of one blade. That is a judgement until the bench test measures it.
  • Gear mesh. A printed module 0.8 pair needs its centre distance right to 0.1 mm. Print the housing with the two shaft holes in one piece.
  • The cable in the mast. The camera's own cable must fit in the blades' lens (about 5 mm at its middle) and survive the coil's flexing. Check its diameter when it arrives; if it is too thick, use four thin wires with the data pair twisted.
  • Snagging. The head must not catch a pipe or hanger wire while the drone yaws. The yaw runs slowly and stops when the autofocus reads under 10 cm.
  • Hover time. The probe is about 61 g, and dropping the runners and keel saved more, so the Scout is about 702 g all-up (E), with about 2 minutes of hover per opening.
  • Parking. The parked head is the highest point of the Scout. The TPU guard on the board's rim takes an upside-down landing first.

The probe touches nothing below the deck, so the funnel seat and the battery swapper do not change.
