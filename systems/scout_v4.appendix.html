How ceiling voids are inspected today

Nobody flies a drone into a drop-ceiling void as a routine product. Inspectors push a camera up from below, crawl a robot across the tiles, or fly a large caged drone in open industrial ceilings. Each one teaches something for the Scout.

What the Scout's ceiling demands

The void

Suspended ceilings hang on a grid of 600 mm (2 ft) tiles. The void above is typically 150–600 mm deep, and 600–900 mm in ducted offices (clearance guide). It holds sprinkler mains and branches, ducts, conduit with couplings and junction boxes, cable trays, and the hanger wires that hold the grid. It is dark and dusty.

The ceiling effect

A rotor close under a surface gains thrust. Research finds the effect smaller and steadier than ground effect (indoor airflow study), but a flight controller that holds altitude keeps adding thrust when the drone stops climbing, and pilots report quads being sucked onto the ceiling (forum report). Prop wash also lifts loose tiles. Spark's call is that the Scout can fly close, so it holds its roof 120 mm below the grid. Its props are then 137 mm below, about one prop diameter, where the effect is noticeable but modest, and the mast does the rest of the reaching.

Where a mast can stand

Every part of the Scout keeps 12 mm from the swept 5.1 in prop discs. A vertical mast clears all four only within about 24 mm of the drone's centre on the diagonals, and 43 mm on the axes. The centre is also where yawing spins the head in place. So the probe lives in the flat roof's middle node, with its spool hanging under it above the FC. At its corners the spool is 15 mm from the discs.

Weight and money

The Scout is already about 735 g (E) on 5.1 in props. Spark's limit for this probe is cheap and light. The design keeps every part but the camera under $16 and adds no second pan axis, because the drone's own yaw is a free pan axis.

What the camera records

The camera is the one Spark bought: the Arducam 12MP autofocus USB camera (B0FLX58WKZ). It is a standard USB video camera, so the Radxa Zero 3W's Linux takes it with no driver. It flies in its metal case, which is its heat spreader (Spark: "keep the case on"), with the case's bracket taken off: about 45 g. The probe runs about a minute in every ten, so the case is plenty.

One spool or two? The mast, simulated (9 Nov)

Spark doubted the probe: "two bends fighting when retracting", and asked to simulate the design as far as we can, find out whether one spool works or we need two, and draw the mechanism to its final version. Six studies follow: plate theory of the coil, the stroke's torque, a discrete elastica of the coil's outer turn, a CalculiX shell model of the lens, and the cable. Every number is E until Spark measures the blade (t and its cup radius). The scripts are tools/probe_mast_sim.py and tools/probe_mast_fe.py.

Decision: one spool, redrawn. The fight is real, but it is about length, not the blades' curls. The head's wires lie between the two blades on the coil, so the outer blade pays out more per turn: 17.3 mm over the stroke on the 9 Nov design, against the clip's ±3 mm slot. Two spools would end it, but they need about 75 mm across where the props' keep-out leaves about 40, and both blades would coil the hard way (+29 % torque). So it stays one spool, with these changes: a Ø28 hub (Ø20 put the blade at 1574 MPa, near yield); a 0.6 mm ribbon of 30 AWG PTFE wire (the 26 AWG pair made it 1.2); the fight cut to 7.5 mm and taken by an 11 mm slot in a 14 mm sleeve at the head; a sprung pressure roller that stops the coil blossoming; and the 100 rpm N20, because the 300 rpm stalls below what winding in needs.

1 · The coil: energy, unwind torque, stress

One 19 mm blade, flattened and coiled, stores 4.0 N·mm per mm at r 10 coiled equal-sense (cup toward the hub, like a tape measure) and 7.1 coiled opposite-sense. That is also the push it gives to deploy itself. At r 14 the figures are 2.6 and 4.7. Its unwind torque on the spool is almost the same at any radius, about 36 N·mm equal-sense and 66 opposite: the motor must hold about 100 N·mm on a two-blade spool all the time. Von Mises at the surface is 1476 and 1574 MPa at r 10 (yield of hardened tape steel about 1600, E), and 1197 and 1283 at r 14.

The smallest hub at 90 % of yield depends steeply on the blade's thickness. At t 0.13 it is r 11.5 opposite-sense; at 0.15 it is r 14.8; at 0.17 it is r 19.6 (cup R 15). The hub is now r 14. Spark: measure the blade's thickness (ten layers with calipers ÷ 10) and the cup's depth across its 19 mm. If t is over 0.14, the hub grows.

2 · One spool or two

On one spool the outer blade (A, cup toward the hub) coils equal-sense and the inner one (B) opposite-sense. They do not push each other apart: a flattened blade curls slightly toward its own cup (ν/R, a radius of 50 mm), so both curl toward each other and press the ribbon between them. What does fight is length. Everything between the two blades on the coil spaces them apart, so A pays out 2π × (t + ribbon) more than B on every turn: 17.3 mm with the 1.2 bundle on the Ø20 hub, 7.5 with the 0.6 ribbon on the Ø28 hub, and 1.9 with nothing between them.

Two spools side by side, with the blades leaving from the inner tangents, coil identically, so no length fight, but both blades are opposite-sense. Both equal-sense is impossible in a plane: each blade's coil has to lie on the side its cup faces, which is the other blade's side, so the paths would cross unless an S-bend over rollers adds an opposite-sense bend anyway.

What decides it is space. Over z 28–60 the props' keep-out (disc + 4 + 12) leaves |x| ≤ 20 at |y| 15. One Ø31.8 spool fits. Two spools geared 1:1 need about 75 mm across and cross the keep-out by about 15 mm on each side.

3 · Torque over the stroke

Paying out, the coil pushes itself out and the motor holds it back; winding in, the motor drives against the coil. The friction where the throat re-forms the cup is the unknown, so each case is plotted at μ 0.05 and 0.3 (about 8.5 N on the walls per blade over a 30 mm transition). On one spool with the r 14 hub, the motor holds 12–81 N·mm while paying out and drives 106–173 N·mm winding in. Two spools would be 136–196. The 300 rpm N20 the parts list has stalls at about 140 N·mm on the spool through 40 / 12, and a 1:236 worm N20 at about 167 (both E). Neither is enough.

The chosen drive is the same N20 body at 100 rpm (B07MZH851L): about 420 N·mm at stall (E), 2.4 times the worst case winding in, which is the drone inverted at μ 0.3. That is 1.6 turns in about 4.5 s. Parked, the DRV8833 brakes the motor and the head stays seated: the coil pushes with about 35 N·mm at the gearbox's output. A constant-force spring would cancel the coil's push, but it has no room inside the keep-out.

4 · Blossoming

This is a discrete elastica of the coil's outer turn and its free span up the throat's slot: frictionless contact with the coil, a housing 1.5 mm out and the slot. With nothing holding the coil, the outer turn stays down only while the free span pulls more than EI k²/2, which is 3.1 N for the stack. Any push makes it spring out to the housing and draw about 7 mm of mast back in. Paying out, the mast is pushed (the head and the throat's friction) while re-forming the cup in the throat pushes 1.7 N per blade out, so a hub-driven coil blossoms unless it is locked. A pressure roller locks it: friction under it holds the turns together, and the arc from the roller to the exit stays down under a 3 N push. The roller needs (push − 3.4 + 3.1) / μ: about 6 N for a 2 N push at μ 0.3. It is an Ø8 tube on two MR63ZZ, 30° before the exit, pushed by two springs at 12 N on the full coil and 7.8 N empty. Winding in, the span is in tension and the coil stays tight on its own.

A pinch-roller drive at the throat would feed the coil in compression while winding in. It only works with a take-up spring on the spool stronger than the coil's ~100 N·mm push, and that spring has no room either. So the spool is driven at its hub.

5 · The lens: CalculiX shell model

The blades are modelled in CalculiX 2.21 (bricks one layer thick through the blade, which is what it makes of shells; nonlinear geometry). The lens edges are unbonded: frictionless gap elements every 2 mm, free to slide and open. One flattened blade gets back 90 % of its cup within 25 mm and 95 % within 30 (t 0.13, R 15; 21–33 mm over t 0.11–0.19 and R 12.7–25.4). The pair, flattened together, needs 30 / 35 mm. The 32 mm throat therefore delivers the lens at 90 % or more of its depth, and the last 5 % forms in the first 3 mm above it. The throat stays at 32; a longer one would lift the head again.

Across its thickness (x), the unbonded lens is exactly two single blades: the edges slide along each other, so it is 0.67 N/mm at the head and folds at about 0.50 N·m. That is 2.5 times the 0.2 N·m bench target and 4.5 times the 0.11 N·m a 1 g sideways load of the head puts on its base. Across its width it is 36 times stiffer and folds at 3.6 N·m. One blade alone folds at 0.10 in −x, under the target: this is why there are two. It buckles under 53–82 N against the head's 0.59 N. Gluing the edges would make it 6 times stiffer in x, but then the blades could not flatten onto the spool. The turret's throat now carries the mast's fold moments: 0.6 N·m in x and 1.2 in y (was 0.3). The fold runs stop where the solver loses the path at the snap, so they are the last converged peaks; the +x unbonded peak moves ±23 % with the mesh, and ≈ 0.50 matches −x. *21 N·m would yield the steel first.

6 · The cable

The lens is 17.8 mm across and 5.8 deep. The ribbon, six 30 AWG PTFE wires flat (4.9 × 0.81, 12 Nov: the type E wire's 0.032 in OD; was 3.3 × 0.6), lies against A inside it. Between the blades on the coil it pays out halfway between them, about 4.7 mm less than A, so a loop under the sleeve's top takes it up; blade B pays out 9.5 mm less than A, taken by its M2 in the head's 12 mm slot (10 of travel). The USB pair runs side by side with a ground on each side; on 0.4 m that is an E for USB 2.0 high speed, and the bench test decides. 5 V and ground in 30 AWG drop about 0.1 V at 0.5 A over the round trip. The 470 µF at the servo covers its start.

What changed, and what Spark measures

  • Spool: hub Ø28 (was Ø20), 1.6 turns of the stack to Ø30.8, Ø31.8 flanges; the axis stays (0, y, 41). The mast is at x 15.1 (was 12.5), the wound stack's tangent.
  • Ribbon: 6 × 30 AWG PTFE (Remington 30KITSTRPTFE, B0722LL645), 0.6 thick, replaces the 26 / 30 AWG bundle.
  • Head: a 14 mm sleeve round the lens. A is held by an M2 at its top; B's M2 and nut ride an 11 mm slot; the ribbon has a 4 mm loop. The head sits 8 mm higher.
  • Roller: new. An Ø8 × 19 printed tube on two MR63ZZ (uxcell B075CLH2WM), its M3 axle in radial slots of the turret, two 4 × 0.5 × 15 springs (uxcell B076LQCBR1).
  • Motor: N20 6 V 100 rpm (B07MZH851L) in place of the 300 rpm. Same body and pocket, same 12 / 40 gears.
  • To buy: the 100 rpm N20, the MR63ZZ, the springs, the PTFE wire. To measure: the blade's thickness and cup depth; the N20's stall torque (a weight on a 10 mm arm); the friction of blade on blade.

Coupon: what to report (12 Nov)

Spark (12 Nov): "only have me do what is absolutely necessary". Nothing gets measured. The coupon answers whatever no listing could, and each test has three choices labelled on the print. Send back one line, e.g. D2 B1 A3 G2 M1 T2 C2. That line goes into COUPON_PICK in tools/scout_v4_shape.py, and the spool, pinion, roller, bench turret and the flight turret, head frame and cradle are remade from it.

  1. D, B, A: press the N20's shaft into D1–D3, an MR63ZZ into B1–B3 and an M3 screw into A1–A3. Report the D that grips firm, the B that holds the bearing upside down, and the smallest A that turns free with no wobble.
  2. G: set the printed spool on each peg pair with the pinion beside it, and spin. Report the first pair that turns with no tight spot.
  3. M: wrap a 10 cm scrap of blade once round M1, then M2, then M3, with its curved-in (marked) face out. Hold each 30 s and let go. Report the smallest that leaves no curl.
  4. T: put the two blades together, cups facing, with the ribbon between them. Push them up through T1–T3 from the flat end (hold the coupon up). Report the narrowest they pass without binding.
  5. C: stand the camera on edge in the C1–C3 gaps, front to back across the gap. Report the narrowest it drops into.

Estimates in the design until then (E): blade t 0.13 (tape patents: 0.11–0.15), cup R 15 (12.7–25.4), the ribbon 0.81 thick and 4.9 wide (30 AWG PTFE, MIL-W-16878/4 type E, OD 0.032 in), N20 stall 2.2 kg·cm (vendors: 1.5–2.2), SG90 tab holes 27.7 (oval pilots take ±0.4), camera case 20 deep. The margins over those ranges: the stroke needs 100–292 N·mm against 417–612 at the spool. Hub r 14 stays under 90 % of yield for t up to 0.13 at any cup and for t 0.14 at R ≥ 15. M2 or M3 means a 15.5 or 17 hub.

Assembly order

  1. Heat-set 4 inserts into the bench base; bolt the bench turret on (4 × M3 × 10).
  2. Cut two 200 mm blades. Drill or punch Ø2.2 at the tip ends for the head's M2s, and scuff the paint on the spool ends. Lay the ribbon on blade A and kapton-bind it.
  3. Epoxy the stack's three ends (B on the hub's side) into the spool's slot. Route the ribbon through the hub to the drum hub, then wind it 1.6 turns.
  4. Fit the spool on the M3 × 40 axle. Fit the roller on its M3 × 40 axle, springs behind its ends, and the N20 with its pressed pinion. Feed the stack up the throat.
  5. Fit the head frame's sleeve: A's M2 at the top, B's M2 and nut in the slot. Then the cradle and SG90.
  6. Tests: 20 cycles out and in, timing each stroke and watching the coil (no lift-off), the lens at the throat and B's travel in the slot (expect ~7.5 mm). Then push the head sideways at 150 mm out until it folds, with a luggage scale (expect ~3.3 N in x).

The mast, part by part

The turret sits under the roof's middle node, where the flight controller used to be. The controller moves to a bay under the nose. Everything that has to stand in the props' height band is within 24 mm of the centre, the only place a part stays 12 mm from all four props. The gear motor lies in a pocket in the deck, below that band.

  1. Two blades. Two 19 mm (¾") steel tape blades, two 0.2 m strips cut from one ordinary 3 m tape measure (its case and spring are not used; 0.15 m of each rolls out), with their curves facing. 19 mm rather than 10 (Spark: "as safe as possible"): about 3.5 times the bending moment to fold them, for about 5 g more, so the mast holds the camera even in its case. Out of the throat they spring into a lens-shaped tube, and each braces the direction the other folds easily. This is the bi-STEM idea from spacecraft booms. The flat cable runs inside the lens, protected. The reach is as short as still works (Spark: "as safe as possible"): halving it from 0.3 m makes the tip about 8 times stiffer, raises the load that buckles it about 4 times and halves the bending at the throat, and the lens still ends about 7.5 cm above the grid, roughly 6 cm over the tiles' tops.
  2. One spool. Both blades and the ribbon of head wires wind flat together on one printed spool: hub Ø28 (Ø20 put the blade at 1574 MPa; the simulations), 23 mm wide, Ø30.8 wound, Ø31.8 flanges, axis across the drone 41 mm above the datum. 1.6 turns pay out the 0.15 m. The outer blade pays out 7.5 mm more than the inner over the stroke; the head's sleeve takes it. A sprung roller 30° before the exit keeps the coil from blossoming while the mast is pushed out.
  3. Drive. An N20 6 V 100 rpm gear motor (the 300 rpm stalls below the ~170 N·mm winding in can need) lies under the spool and the drum, facing across the drone, in a pocket through the deck. It turns the spool through a printed 12 / 40 tooth pair (module 0.8, centres 20.8 mm): about 420 N·mm at stall (E), about 4.5 s for the 0.15 m. Parked, the driver brakes it and the head stays seated.
  4. Cable. The head carries the camera, the servo and the LED bars, so six wires run up the mast as one flat ribbon of 30 AWG PTFE wire (0.6 × 3.3 mm): 5 V, ground, the USB pair (D+ and D−), the servo's signal and the LED bars' switched minus. A JST ZH 1.5 mm 4-pin lead plugs into the camera. The ribbon lies inside the lens, winds between the blades on the spool, and runs through the hub into the drum's loose coil (3.5 turns of room for 1.6). The USB pair goes on to the USB-C breakout on the Radxa.
  5. Throat. A 32 mm printed guide beside the roof's middle node. It takes the blades off the spool and curls them into the lens, and it grips the mast's base, where a knock bends it hardest. The middle node becomes a ring around it; the four roof rods end in the ring's sides.
  6. Head. A 14 mm sleeve round the lens at the blades' tips: blade A is held by an M2 at its top, blade B's M2 rides an 11 mm slot (the 7.5 mm length difference), and the ribbon has a 4 mm loop under its top. On the sleeve sits a small base plate. On its +y side a bracket holds the SG90 by its 32.8 mm tabs, lying on its side with its shaft across the drone; on its -y side a bracket holds an M3 screw on the same line as the idler. The cradle nods -20..+90°.
  7. Light. Two 25 × 8 mm bars cut from a Clearhill 5 V COB strip (4000 K, CRI 90; 25 mm is its cut length), stuck on the camera case's top and bottom faces at the front, facing the way it looks and nodding with it. The metal case is their heat sink and they need no fins. Above and below the lens they fill the shadows on pipes from two sides; 18 mm off the lens's axis, lit dust stays out of the picture; the strip's continuous line gives soft highlights on ducts and copper. At 8 W/m each bar is about 0.2 W, about 50 lux at 0.5 m together (E): the scan holds still, so 1/30 s frames are fine. If the stills are dark, cut 50 mm bars instead (two segments each).

If something hits the mast

  • The two-blade tube is far harder to fold than one blade, the blades are 19 mm wide (about 3.5 times the fold moment of 10 mm ones), and the 30 mm throat holds its base.
  • The TPU guard on the camera board's rim makes a knock glance off before it loads the blades.
  • The yaw runs slowly, and the software stops it when the autofocus says something is within about 10 cm.
  • If the view suddenly jumps, the software rolls the mast in, and the throat straightens the blades on the way down.
  • The blades are turned so that a fold falls along the roof's centre rods, not into an open roof panel over a prop.

A scan at one opening

  1. Fly under the open tile, centred by the flow sensor; the probe's distance sensor sees the opening as far and the tiles as near.
  2. Hold the roof 120 mm below the grid.
  3. Roll the tape out its 150 mm, until the lens is about 75 mm above the grid, LEDs on.
  4. Nod down to 5° and yaw a full turn in 45° steps, holding still about 0.2 s for a 12 MP frame at each; nod up to 45° and turn again; then one frame straight up: 17 frames in about 40 s of hover. The video runs the whole time as well.
  5. Roll the mast back until the head rests on the throat, and fly to the next opening.

From stills to a map

Each still is saved with its yaw, tilt and the autofocus's focus distance. Free photogrammetry software such as COLMAP or Meshroom turns a set into a 3D model of the void, with the focus distances setting its scale. The stills are still worth having without the model: every fitting is in at least two of them.

Wiring and control

The camera goes on the Radxa Zero 3W's USB 3 host port (a USB 2 device there) through the USB-C plug board, whose 5.1 kΩ resistors tell the Radxa it is a device; the Radxa itself is powered on its 5 V pins. The servo's signal comes from a hardware PWM pin (3.3 V, which the SG90 accepts), and three GPIO pins drive the DRV8833: channel A the spool motor both ways, channel B the LED bars' minus to ground (their plus is on the shared 5 V). The scan runs on the Radxa and asks PX4 for each yaw step over MAVLink. 5 V budget from the UBEC: Radxa up to ~1 A, camera ~0.3 A, LEDs 0.08 A, motor up to ~0.5 A at stall, servo over 1 A at each start: about 3 A peak, so the UBEC must give 3 A or more, and the software never starts the servo and the motor at the same moment.

One camera for the whole Scout

Parked, the head rests on the throat with its lens at about (27, −4, 116) mm in the drone's frame. Tilted 15° up, its 61 × 50° frame spans 10° below the horizon to 40° above it, and no rod or prop is in the picture. The servo tilts it up for ceilings.

  • Gained: colour, 12 MP stills with very fine detail, and autofocus from 10 cm, so a fitting right in front of the lens is sharp.
  • Given up: a wide view (61° across, so 17 frames a scan instead of 13), a global shutter (the scan holds still for each frame), and weight: in its case the camera is 45 g, so the probe is about 91 g, which is why the blades went to 19 mm.

How we build it

Each step ends with a test that has to pass before the next step. The mast and the cable are the two unknowns, so they are proven on the bench before anything flies.

  1. Order the parts. Done on 8 Nov, except the JST ZH lead and the 470 µF capacitor; the one 4.9 m tape gives 22 spare strips.
  2. Draw the mechanism parts in the repo. The spool with its gear, the pinion, the drum, the housing and throat, the cradle and the clip are fits and slots, not loaded structure, so each is a drawn part with its own make.py, like the Overseer's TPU foot. Print them in PCTG, and the lip and bumper in TPU 95A.
  3. Build the mast on the bench. Cut two 0.2 m strips from the tape with tin snips, round the corners and file the burrs (the blade is hardened: do not drill it), set one end of each in the spool hub's printed clamp slot with a drop of epoxy, curves facing, and fit the gears and the N20. Pass: 500 roll-outs with no jam, the lens fully formed above the throat, and a 60 g dummy head (the camera in its case) at 0.15 m settling within a second after a tap. Then push the head sideways with a luggage scale until the mast folds. Pass: at least 0.2 N·m at the throat, about 1.3 N at the head, three times the worst load from the drone's tilting and jerking with the case on.
  4. Prove the cable. Make the thin cable (JST ZH lead, four thin wires, the data pair twisted, soldered to the USB-C board), run it between the blades, round the spool and into the drum's coil, and plug the camera into the Radxa through it. Stream 1080p for 10 minutes while the mast cycles 100 times. Pass: no USB drop-out and no chafe. If it drops out, use the camera's own 1 m cable shortened instead.
  5. Assemble the head. Take the bracket off the camera's case, measure the case's depth and where the cable socket sits, and fit the cradle's walls and strap to it; then fit the servo with its capacitor and the LED bars. Pass: autofocus sharp at 0.1, 0.5 and 1 m, and a usable frame in a dark box with the LEDs on.
  6. Write the probe service on the Radxa. Retract until the motor stalls with the head seated, extend until it stalls at the blades' anchor (time-limited, about 1 s each way), stop the yaw when the autofocus reads under 10 cm, and save each still with its yaw, tilt and focus distance. When parked, stream the front view.
  7. Write the scan. The Radxa asks PX4 over MAVLink for each 60° yaw step and waits for it to settle before each still. Run it in PX4's simulator first, then on the bench with the props off.
  8. Fit it to v4. The roof's middle node is designed with the turret's mount when the cage nodes are designed. Bolt the turret on, wire it to the Radxa and the UBEC, weigh the Scout and check the centre of gravity.
  9. Fly under a test ceiling. Hang a 2 × 2 tile grid with one tile open over a frame. Hover 30 cm under it, then 20, then 12, logging thrust and altitude. Pass: no pull toward the grid. Then roll the tape out in the hover, run a full scan, and build a COLMAP model from the stills.
  10. Fly a real ceiling, one opening first.

What to prove first

  • Ceiling effect at 12 cm. The props are about one diameter below the grid. Test the hover there first. If PX4 starts climbing into the ceiling, hold a few centimetres lower and roll the tape out further.
  • Folding. The two-blade tube should take several times the push of one blade. That is a judgement until the bench test measures it.
  • Gear mesh. A printed module 0.8 pair needs its centre distance right to 0.1 mm. Print the housing with the two shaft holes in one piece.
  • The cable in the mast. Four thin wires carry USB 2.0 up the mast and through the coil. Twist the data pair and keep the run short (about 0.6 m); the fallback is the camera's own cable, shortened.
  • Snagging. The head must not catch a pipe or hanger wire while the drone yaws. The yaw runs slowly and stops when the autofocus reads under 10 cm.
  • Hover time. The probe is about 91 g with the camera in its case; dropping the runners and keel saved more than that, so the Scout is about 732 g all-up (E), still under v3's 765 g, with about 2 minutes of hover per opening.
  • Parking. The parked head is the highest point of the Scout. The TPU guard on the board's rim takes an upside-down landing first.

The probe touches nothing below the deck, so the funnel seat and the battery swapper do not change.
