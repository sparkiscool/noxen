Lantern morph frame

The LiDAR rides a boom that swings over the drone like a lantern on a pole. The four arms sweep between an X and a narrow line. One rule ties them together: the LiDAR's base plane always has every propeller behind it, so it sees its whole hemisphere, all around, in every pose the mechanism can reach.

Why a swing boom

Seeing up needs the LiDAR above every prop; seeing down needs it below every prop. The hard part is the path between. Three ways to carry one LiDAR from the top to the bottom, drawn at the moment that decides them:

What moves

  1. Arm sweep, one degree of freedom. Four arms on vertical pivots at the hub corners. The front pair sweeps forward, the rear pair back, mirrored by a single scissor link so one servo sets the sweep. Left arms sit one stagger above the right arms; in the narrow line the left and right disks overlap in plan and pass over each other.
  2. Boom swing, one degree of freedom. A hollow boom on a hinge along the x axis through the hub centre, worm-driven so it holds any angle without power. The LiDAR sits on its end, axis along the boom, cable inside. Up: scans the ceiling and all around. Down: scans the floor and all around. The boom plane x = 0 is the gap between front and rear props at every sweep.
  3. The gate, no degree of freedom. A plate on the boom hinge carries the green shape from the map as a window; a pin on the sweep slider rides in it. Wide arms allow the boom only near up or down. Pull the arms in and the boom may swing over. The clearance is a fact of the hardware, not of the flight code.

What it buys

  • One LiDAR, full 360° azimuth, whole hemisphere, nothing of the drone in it, in every pose.
  • Width shrinks to about half between pipes and joists; the boom then lifts the LiDAR a boom length above the frame into a roof cavity, or drops it the same below between pipes.
  • Wide X in open air for gust margin, narrow line indoors, chosen in flight.

What it asks of a real build

  • Overlapping props. In the narrow line the left and right disks overlap heavily; expect a thrust loss of roughly a fifth there, and keep the narrow line for transits.
  • Boom load. The LiDAR mass at the end of the boom is a bending load on the hinge and a moment on the flight controller; the readout shows the holding torque at horizontal. A worm gear or a brake holds it; the servo only moves it.
  • Landing. Legs hang under the motors. The frame lands with the boom up or wide-and-up; a down pose is a flight pose only.
  • Centre of gravity. The CG moves a few centimetres with the boom; the flight controller must take the pose as an input.
  • Inertia. Sweeping the arms changes the yaw and roll inertia by a large factor; the attitude gains must follow the sweep.
  • Cables. Four motor leads pass the arm pivots; the LiDAR cable passes the boom hinge. Both need a slip loop sized for the full travel.

Nothing here is a bought part yet. Sizes default to today's props and the L2 so the numbers mean something, and every size on the sheet is a slider.

Field model: anything within the LiDAR's hemisphere plus 6° below its base plane is lit; the check adds a safety below that line. Frame points checked: 48 on each prop rim, 12 on each motor skirt, each leg tip, each arm tube top and bottom at three stations, eight hub corners. Elevation angles are measured in 3D from the LiDAR base centre. Prop disks are flat planes at motor height plus 20 mm. No prop cone, no flex, no gust.
